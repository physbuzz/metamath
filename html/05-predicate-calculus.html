5.0 Important Notations, Terminology, and Citations

5.0.1 Variables in which language?

There is an easy way to get confused before we've even started: the word "variable" is doing several jobs. In an ordinary first-order formula, a variable can occur as an argument of a predicate or be bound by a quantifier. In a textbook's discussion of those formulas, a metavariable can stand for a whole formula. Metamath then has its own declared variables and type hypotheses. I want to keep track of which of these we're discussing, instead of relying on the typography to tell us.

Source 1. The Metamath book, especially §1.4.5, "Metamath and First-Order Logic," and §4.2.4, "The $d Statement." The latter starts on printed page 120. The important point for our purposes is that the verifier does not have a primitive free-variable or bound-variable test. Its substitution mechanism and $d conditions are used by an axiom system to control the substitutions that are permitted.

For example, ph has type wff in our familiar database, while x has type setvar in the predicate-calculus portion of set.mm. Both are Metamath variables. An instance of ph may be a large formula containing several setvar variables; an instance of x must satisfy its own syntax hypothesis. The declaration vx $f setvar x $. belongs to the syntax machinery. It is not itself a quantified assertion about a mathematical set.

This also explains why a condition involving x and ph can make sense. We are restricting which replacements for two Metamath variables can be used together. The Proof Explorer's discussion of axioms, schemes, and distinct metavariables is useful to read alongside the book here.

5.0.2 Free occurrences, bound occurrences, and capture

Source 2. Harper's Practical Foundations for Programming Languages, Chapter 1. His treatment of abstract syntax and binding gives us terminology for talking about these problems outside a particular Metamath database. The sustained reading of Harper is going in the separate O5.5 notebook; here I just want enough vocabulary to describe the examples in front of us.

Consider the familiar formula

$$x=y\ \to\ \forall x\,(x=y).$$𝑥=𝑦 → ∀𝑥(𝑥=𝑦).

The occurrence of x on the left is free, while the occurrence of x inside the quantified equality is bound. The quantifier does not reach back across the implication. This is why it is useful to talk about occurrences: one written variable name can have different roles in different parts of a formula.

A substitution can change these roles if we're careless. For example, consider replacing the free x in

$$\forall y\,(x=y)$$∀𝑦(𝑥=𝑦)

by y. Simply changing the printed letter gives

$$\forall y\,(y=y).$$∀𝑦(𝑦=𝑦).

The newly inserted occurrence is now inside the scope of the y quantifier. This is the sort of phenomenon called variable capture. A treatment of capture-avoiding substitution must say how it handles this situation; in a named-variable presentation, renaming a bound variable is one way to make the required room.

Two expressions that differ only by a suitable change of bound-variable names are called alpha-equivalent in the usual binding terminology. I'll use that name when discussing the syntax. For the actual predicate-calculus exercises, we will prove particular logical equivalences using named set.mm theorems. Those are concrete things to check, and they give us something to compare with the general discussion in Harper.

5.0.3 Tarski and the distinct-variable approach

Source 3. The Proof Explorer's history of distinct variables. It credits Tarski with stating the approach in 1951, with proofs published in 1965. The historical point I want to remember is the possibility of organizing predicate calculus around comparatively elementary distinct-variable restrictions, while deriving the more elaborate behavior within the system.

The comment on ax-5 gives a direct connection to Tarski's system. It asserts

$$\vdash\bigl(\varphi\to\forall x\,\varphi\bigr),$$⊢(𝜑→∀𝑥𝜑),

with the condition that x does not occur in the substituted formula. In Metamath source, that restriction is written $d x ph $.. This is a good historical example to keep attached to an actual axiom we will use, instead of remembering only that Tarski did something clever with variables.

There is a tradeoff to notice: making the verifier's primitive operation small does not make every familiar logical manipulation a primitive operation. Changing bound variables, specializing a quantified formula, or transporting an equivalence still needs the relevant assertions and their proofs. That is the machinery we will borrow and practice using in this chapter.

5.0.4 What $d asks the checker to do

The exact rule in §4.2.4 of the book has two parts. When applying an assertion with a required disjoint pair, the two replacement expressions must have no Metamath variables in common. Also, every pair consisting of one variable from each replacement must be covered by the active disjoint conditions in the proof. The second part is how the restriction is inherited.

For a small example, suppose an assertion requires $d ph ps $., and consider the replacements ph by x = y and ps by z = w. The variables occurring in the two replacements are disjoint as written. The proof still needs the cross-pairs x,z, x,w, y,z, and y,w. It does not acquire a requirement that x and y be distinct from each other from this particular $d pair. We're checking pairs across the two replacements.

If instead both replacements contain y, there is an immediate conflict. Declaring extra pairs cannot repair that overlap. On the other hand, identical replacements containing only constants do not conflict with this particular test, because they contain no variables. Their syntax hypotheses still have to be satisfied, of course. These are checks on substitution data, so we can practice them before making a large predicate-calculus proof.

The discussion attached to 19.21v also gives a useful terminology distinction: "does not occur" is stronger than "is not free in." For instance, x is not free in A. x x = x, but it certainly occurs there. That formula therefore cannot be used as the replacement for ph in a direct application of ax-5 with x as the bound variable. There are other theorems for less restrictive situations.

5.1 Predicate Calculus in set.mm

5.1.1 The starting machinery

We'll keep the useful propositional theorems from chapter 4 and borrow the remaining propositional helpers as needed. The syntax declarations for equivalence and conjunction are wb and wa; their definitions are df-bi and df-an. The syntax and defining assertions are $a statements. Supporting rules such as impbii, which assembles an equivalence from its two directions, retain their $p proofs.

The other starting material includes quantifier syntax, the predicate-calculus axioms needed by the supplied lemmas, and the definition of the existential quantifier. In particular, df-ex relates existential quantification to negation and universal quantification. We can inspect that definition when we reach the existential exercises.

5.1.3 Putting a quantifier around an implication or equivalence

Now for the question about manipulating something inside a formula. The rules alimi and albii let us carry an established implication or equivalence under a universal quantifier:

$$\frac{\vdash(\varphi\to\psi)} {\vdash(\forall x\,\varphi\to\forall x\,\psi)} \quad\texttt{alimi},$$⊢(𝜑→𝜓)⊢(∀𝑥𝜑→∀𝑥𝜓)𝚊𝚕𝚒𝚖𝚒,
$$\frac{\vdash(\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi)} {\vdash(\forall x\,\varphi\leftrightarrow\forall x\,\psi)} \quad\texttt{albii}.$$⊢(𝜑↔𝜓)⊢(∀𝑥𝜑↔∀𝑥𝜓)𝚊𝚕𝚋𝚒𝚒.

As in chapter 4, the displayed rules omit their syntax hypotheses. Neither rule requires a $d x ph $. condition. Their premises are established implications or equivalences; this is different from treating an arbitrary formula as true only for some temporarily chosen value of x.

metamath
${
  alimi.1 $e |- ( ph -> ps ) $.
  alimi $p |- ( A. x ph -> A. x ps ) $= ? $.
$}

${
  albii.1 $e |- ( ph <-> ps ) $.
  albii $p |- ( A. x ph <-> A. x ps ) $= ? $.
$}

The first proof should be a small exercise with ax-gen and ax-4. The second can use the supplied equivalence machinery. I want to recognize this pattern well enough to use it later: prove the relation between the inner formulas, then apply the theorem that transports it through the surrounding construction.

5.1.5 Vacuous quantifiers and existential quantification

A quantifier whose variable does not occur in the formula gives us two more useful targets, 19.3v and 19.9v:

metamath
${
  $d x ph $.
  19.3v $p |- ( A. x ph <-> ph ) $= ? $.
  19.9v $p |- ( E. x ph <-> ph ) $= ? $.
$}

For the universal case, the two directions have different justifications. ax-5 introduces the quantifier under the stated restriction. A supplied specialization theorem removes it. We'll use the existing specialization machinery here; deriving all of it from the primitive predicate-calculus axioms can be a separate exercise if I decide I want one.

The existential definition in set.mm is

$$\vdash\bigl(\exists x\,\varphi\leftrightarrow \neg\forall x\,\neg\varphi\bigr) \quad\texttt{df-ex}.$$⊢(∃𝑥𝜑↔¬∀𝑥¬𝜑)𝚍𝚏-𝚎𝚡.

This gives us a connection to the negation rules from chapter 4. Another natural target is eximi:

metamath
${
  eximi.1 $e |- ( ph -> ps ) $.
  eximi $p |- ( E. x ph -> E. x ps ) $= ? $.
$}

The statement has the same overall pattern as alimi. As a small application, use it with a conjunction-elimination theorem to obtain an existentially quantified implication from ph /\ ps to ph. The useful point is seeing the old propositional result become an input to the new quantifier rule.

$$\vdash\bigl(\forall x\,(x=z)\leftrightarrow \forall y\,(y=z)\bigr),$$⊢(∀𝑥(𝑥=𝑧)↔∀𝑦(𝑦=𝑧)),

$$\vdash\bigl(x=y\to(\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi)\bigr)$$⊢(𝑥=𝑦→(𝜑↔𝜓))

5.4 Quiz Questions

These questions are meant to test the terminology and the mechanics that the proof exercises use. The answers below are short sketches to revise after working through the chapter.

Question 1

In ( x = y -> A. x x = y ), which occurrences of x and y are free, and which are bound? Why is it more precise to ask about occurrences than simply to classify each variable name as free or bound?

Answer sketch

The x in the left equality is free, and the x in the right equality is bound by A. x; the x written immediately after A. names the binder. Both equality occurrences of y are free. A single name can occur in different scopes; the binding depends on the occurrence's location.

Question 2

An assertion requires $d ph ps $.. We replace ph by x = y and ps by z = w. Which inherited pairs are needed? Why isn't the fact that these are four differently written letters enough?

Answer sketch

The cross-pairs are x,z, x,w, y,z, and y,w. The checker needs the corresponding active restrictions. The distinct spellings by themselves do not record which later substitutions the assertion permits.

Question 3

Explain why $d x y $. is different from |- -. x = y. Also explain why $d x ph $. is stronger than saying that x is not free in the formula.

Answer sketch

The first is a restriction on substitutions for Metamath variables; the second is a mathematical assertion of inequality. For the formula case, nonoccurrence excludes bound occurrences too. A. x x = x is a concrete example in which x occurs but is not free.

Question 4

What is variable capture? Describe what goes wrong with the naive replacement of free x by y in A. y x = y, and explain the role that changing the bound name can play in avoiding it.

Answer sketch

The inserted y falls within the scope of the existing A. y binder and becomes bound. A suitable change to a fresh bound name can preserve the role of the inserted occurrence. The admissibility of the change must be handled by the chosen syntax rules or, in our exercises, by an appropriate theorem.

Question 5

What is the historical connection between Tarski's work and Metamath's distinct-variable approach? Name the source used for the dates.

Answer sketch

The Proof Explorer's history credits Tarski with stating the approach in 1951 and publishing proofs in 1965. The relevant idea is organizing an axiomatization using distinct-variable conditions rather than requiring the usual free-variable and proper-substitution machinery as primitive notions.

Question 6

Compare ax-gen with ax-5. Why can't we simply turn the former into an unrestricted theorem |- ( ph -> A. x ph )?

Answer sketch

ax-gen takes an established assertion as a premise and universally quantifies it. The closed implication has a different logical claim. ax-5 supplies that implication under a disjoint-variable restriction, and the ax-gen commentary explicitly warns that its unrestricted closed form need not hold.

Question 7

Which tool would you look for if you had proved ph <-> ps and wanted A. x ph <-> A. x ps? What changes if you instead have ph -> ps and want ph -> A. x ps?

Answer sketch

The first is the pattern of albii. The second is the pattern of alrimiv, whose disjointness condition constrains ph and x. In the first case both sides are quantified; in the second the antecedent remains outside.

Question 8

In the rename-equality exercise, why do we need to protect z as well as distinguish x and y? What would a general theorem about alpha-renaming say beyond this particular checked equivalence?

Answer sketch

Substituting the two equality formulas into cbvalvw produces the additional pairs x,z and y,z. A general theorem about a specified renaming procedure would quantify over represented expressions and the procedure's inputs or outputs. This exercise supplies one checked instance using an existing rule.
