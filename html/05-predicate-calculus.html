Introduction.

(I should emphasize to logicians reading this section that we're coming at the issue from the perspective of Metamath tinkerers with total disregard for mathematics, we just want to express fun concepts and we don't care how we twist Metamath to get there. So I hope that explains some of the weird perspective from time to time. I do try to bend over backwards to cite stuff and follow conventions of the literature though.)

Before getting to predicate calculus, we should spend a bit of time seeing how far we can get "without quantifiers." This question and related ones such as "why do we need quantifiers?" takes us quite deep into metamathematics and the debates of the early 1900's. I wasn't even trying to get there, I was just confused, and certain I'd turn the corner and be hit with "aha! So that's why we need quantifiers."

We run into a problem immediately: what do we even mean, concretely, by "with quantifiers" or "without quantifiers"?

The quantifier axioms in set.mm use disjoint variable restrictions, so maybe the right framing is whether or not $d is allowed? Nope, MPE appendix 7 describes how to drop some of the axioms from set.mm such that we have predicate logic with no disjoint variable restrictions (though the appendix does state that we have to build other machinery to take its place).

From what I can gather, at this point in a truly obstinate Metamath-first approach, we don't have the tools or terminology to motivate quantifiers for any other reason than "we [mathematicians] often phrase proofs in them" or "the ZFC axioms have quantifiers in them." If we're given an obfuscated Metamath database with the variables all renamed, we can't easily determine whether it "uses quantifiers" or not, unless we do archaeological work or match the axioms directly to a publication. I really dislike this kind of history-first, human-sociology-first statement, but I think it's the best we've got.

Reasons to include quantifiers, appeal to emotion:

  • Axioms and statements containing variables are already a sort of "for all" statement. However, we find we can't include "not for all" statements.
  • We also might want to have a $\forall$∀ statement in an assumption, which can't be done with the tools we have so far. (Here I just mean that, in our databases, a statement like mp.min $e |- P $. doesn't mean "hypothesize that for all P, turnstile P," rather it can be satisfied by any one wff P and |- P.)
  • We might want nested $\forall$∀ statements.

Essentially, we have a notion of $\forall$∀ at the top level of a statement, but we can't do anything where we have to bring $\forall$∀ inside the statement. TODO: I'm still reading Frege's Begriffsschrift, I believe this is the motivation given in there.

Finer points of quantifiers, appeal to Platonic ideals which we don't yet have the tools to prove/discuss:

  • Hilbert and Bernays' first epsilon theorem states that in certain very general cases, theorems whose proofs involve quantifiers can be rewritten as proofs not involving quantifiers. However, it is not general enough for me to easily make a statement like, "all theorems in any Metamath database of such-and-such form can be rewritten so as to not rely on the quantifier axioms," because "any Metamath database" is too broad of a thing for me to constrain. The proof of the theorem does rely on an algorithm to transform proofs, so it's very constructive and concrete and could be a fun project. But it doesn't give us strong no-go theorems of the form "this new system you're writing down contains no new theorems" because it's so easy to write axioms in Metamath which land outside its purview.
  • The ideas of Primitive Recursive Arithmetic were developed by Skolem in 1923 with the explicit goal of being quantifier-free. (Skolem's work was later made more precise and expanded upon, so we can't say that the full formal system is in that 1923 paper.)
  • Quantifiers can be viewed as left- and right- adjoints to substitution. This is far outside the realm where I can comment, but it tells me that if we were in a different universe where we chose a different foundation than Metamath, we might have quantifiers beautifully fall out of our theory right about now. So we're not saying quantifiers aren't special, merely that there is nothing special to motivate them in our notes right now.

Okay, I tried to quarantine all of the things that I cannot concretely formalize into the above writing. There's lots of tantalizing stuff, but let's try to illustrate things concretely with examples now.

5.0 Arithmetic With and Without Quantifiers

This section contains no exercises. We're going to start with the theorems developed in the last chapter; so we have all of propositional logic at our disposal, and all of the useful theorems and lemmas we want.

And I really mean we have all of propositional logic. In the file code-04/prop.mm, which is lines 1 through 12192 of set.mm, we have 1542 proofs of propositional logic. Basically, we can use $\rightarrow,$→, $\leftrightarrow,$↔, $\land,$∧, $\lor,$∨, and $\neg$¬ to our heart's content. Though I don't use them, it also contains nand, xor, and probably other stuff, I wasn't picky.

5.0.3 Induction without quantifiers

Here we're surely forced to use quantifiers, right? Nope!

Mathematical induction is usually phrased as the rule23

$$\frac{\varphi(0)\quad \varphi(x)\to \varphi(Sx)}{\varphi(t)},$$𝜑(0)𝜑(𝑥)→𝜑(𝑆𝑥)𝜑(𝑡),

where somehow we have to treat $\varphi(x)$𝜑(𝑥) as a well-formed formula which is a function of $x.$𝑥. This issue of treating $\varphi$𝜑 as a wff depending on a variable is the first place that I encountered the need for disjoint variables.

Say our well-formed formula is $\varphi,$𝜑, which takes the value t + 0 = t. If we try to "replace $t$𝑡 with zero" in the usual Metamath sense, we'll just unify the whole expression for phi as phi := 0 + 0 = 0. Its status as a formula is lost, and it will no longer be able to be unified with other expressions like our induction hypothesis, ( x + 0 ) = x -> ( S x + 0 ) = S x.

How do we solve this? Let's start with a theorem that "substitutes in" zero for x and shows the formula holds:

Two notes:

  • It's not of much consequence yet, but the trick with defining x as a var makes it so that we can't substitute numbers. I'm purposefully restricting xzero so that it doesn't prove 1 = 0 -> ( 1 + 0 ) = 1.
  • If we had developed our axioms from scratch, here is where we might find we missed a few axioms! For this to work, our axioms had to be powerful enough to allow for interactions like this where we "substitute" the value just by applying axioms.

$$x=t\to (\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi)$$𝑥=𝑡→(𝜑↔𝜓)

5.0.4 Induction with quantifiers

Okay, now how do we introduce quantifiers? There's a few different ways to do this which I'll put in an appendix 5.0.4b. One difficulty of the system we introduced in 5.0.3 is described in Jean van Heijenoort's preface to a translation of Skolem's 1923 paper, where it's mentioned that our version of PRA cannot easily phrase things like the twin prime conjecture, because the "existence" of a pair is difficult to phrase. It's technically possible to state in our language without proving, but would require substantial partial results related to the conjecture. But in first-order logic it's easy to state.

5.0.4.1 Alternate considerations to writing the induction scheme

Our ax6v is Axiom B7 of Tarski, which requires x and y to be distinct. In set.mm the axiom ax-6 has no such condition and ax6v is a theorem that weakens it.

Metamath lists <x,y> as an optional pair in both. A mandatory pair constrains everyone who applies the theorem; an optional pair only constrains the proof. equcomi applies equid and needs no $d at all.
