Introduction.

Before getting to predicate calculus, we should spend a bit of time seeing how far we can get "without quantifiers." This question and related ones such as "why do we need quantifiers?" takes us quite deep into metamathematics and the debates of the early 1900's. I should emphasize to logicians reading this section that we're coming at the issue from the perspective of Metamath tinkerers with total disregard for mathematics, we just want to express fun concepts and we don't care how we twist Metamath to get there. From this perspective, many concepts of mathematical logic look quite strange and difficult to formulate.

We run into a problem immediately: what do we even mean, concretely, by "with quantifiers" or "without quantifiers"?

The quantifier axioms in set.mm use disjoint variable restrictions, so maybe the right framing is whether or not $d is allowed? Nope, MPE appendix 7 describes how to drop some of the axioms from set.mm such that we have predicate logic with no disjoint variable restrictions (though the appendix does state that we have to build other machinery to take its place).

From what I can gather, at this point in a truly obstinate Metamath-first approach, we don't have the tools or terminology to motivate quantifiers for any other reason than "we [mathematicians] often phrase proofs in them" or "the ZFC axioms have quantifiers in them." If we're given a obfuscated Metamath database with the variables all renamed, we can't easily determine whether it "uses quantifiers" or not, unless we do archaeological work or match the axioms directly to a publication. I really dislike this kind of history-first, human-sociology-first statement, but I think it's the best we've got.

Reasons to include quantifiers, appeal to emotion:

  • Axioms and statements containing variables are already a sort of "for all" statement. However, we find we can't include "not for all" statements.
  • We also might want to have a $\forall$∀ statement in an assumption, which can't be done with the tools we have so far. (Here I just mean that, in our databases, a statement like mp.min $e |- P $. doesn't mean "hypothesize that for all P, turnstile P," rather it can be satisfied by any one wff P and |- P.)
  • We might want nested $\forall$∀ statements.

Essentially, we have a notion of $\forall$∀ at the top level of a statement, but we can't do anything where we have to bring $\forall$∀ inside the statement. TODO: I'm still reading Frege's Begriffschrift, I believe this is the motivation given in there.

Finer points of quantifiers, appeal to Platonic ideals which we don't yet have the tools to prove/discuss:

  • Hilbert and Bernays' first epsilon theorem states that in certain very general cases, theorems whose proofs involve quantifiers can be rewritten as proofs not involving quantifiers. However, it is not general enough for me to easily make a statement like, "all theorems in any Metamath database of such-and-such form can be rewritten so as to not rely on the quantifier axioms," because "any Metamath database" is too broad of a thing for me to constrain. The proof of the theorem does rely on an algorithm to transform proofs, so it's very constructive and concrete and could be a fun project. But it doesn't give us strong no-go theorems of the form "this new system you're writing down contains no new theorems" because it's so easy to write axioms in Metamath which land outside its purview.
  • The ideas of Primitive Recursive Arithmetic were developed by Skolem in 1923 with the explicit goal of being quantifier-free. (Skolem's work was later made more precise and expanded upon, so we can't say that the full formal system is in that 1923 paper.)
  • Quantifiers can be viewed as left- and right- adjoints to substitution. This is far outside the realm where I can comment, but it tells me that if we were in a different universe where we chose a different foundation than Metamath, we might have quantifiers beautifully fall out of our theory right about now. So we're not saying quantifiers aren't special, merely that there is nothing special to motivate them in our notes right now.

Okay, I tried to quarantine all of the things that I cannot concretely formalize into the above writing. There's lots of tantalizing stuff, but let's try to illustrate things concretely with examples now.

5.0 Arithmetic With and Without Quantifiers

5.0.1 Common arithmetic axioms

We next can imagine developing arithmetic. 5.0.2 Common arithmetic theorems

After this, we don't really need much ado. We can imagine having a whole host of helper theorems like in propositional logic. A decade ago we might have hemmed and hawed at how long this will take, but in 2026 we know we can knock it using any number of sledgehammers.

  eq-tri $p |- t = v $=

${
  eq-trd.1 $e |- ( ph -> t = u ) $.
  eq-trd.2 $e |- ( ph -> u = v ) $.
  eq-trd $p |- ( ph -> t = v ) $=

    eq-trd.1 nt nu nv eq-tr syl mpd $.

Well, and so on and so forth. But there's one glaring omission that we can't seem to prove... commutativity of addition. Or more pointedly, given the axiom scheme add-0l which says $0 + t = t,$0+𝑡=𝑡, we'd expect to be able to prove the theorem scheme:

metamath
add-0r $p |- ( t + 0 ) = t $= ? $.

In fact, we can't prove this! To say "we can't prove it" requires some justification, though. Number one, this is a metatheorem the likes of which I keep trying and failing to dodge. My concrete, novice perspective is the following: Suppose we introduce some constant num e, such that the axiom or theorem |- -. ( e + 0 ) = e is satisfied. Of course, |- ( 0 + e ) = e also holds, because that's an axiom of system $B$𝐵. If we can prove or argue that this system is consistent, and that the system $B$𝐵 is also consistent, then it can only be the case that |- ( t + 0 ) = t cannot be proved by system $B.$𝐵. Okay, but we can't really broach proofs like that without doing a lot more work or a lot of a different kind of work, so we shelve that. The fact remains that we don't seem to be able to prove add-0r.

But there is indeed an induction argument!

If $(S^n 0+0)=S^n 0$(𝑆𝑛0+0)=𝑆𝑛0, then $S(S^n+0)=S^{n+1}0$𝑆(𝑆𝑛+0)=𝑆𝑛+10 by axiom eq-s. So $(S^{n+1}+0)=S^{n+1}0$(𝑆𝑛+1+0)=𝑆𝑛+10 by add-sl (and probably eq-sym and eq-tr). Also, it is certainly true that $0+0=0$0+0=0 by add-0l. So mathematical induction could prove this handily!

5.0.3 Induction without quantifiers

Here we're surely forced to use quantifiers, right? Nope!

Mathematical induction is usually phrased as the following rule2:

$$\frac{P(0)\quad P(t)\to P(t+1)}{P(u)}$$𝑃(0)𝑃(𝑡)→𝑃(𝑡+1)𝑃(𝑢)

(TODO: check and cite. I know I read good occurrences of induction as used in other books)

For us this is a bit awkward because we haven't built the machinery to define functions. Really what we want to do is something like the following:

Say our well-formed formula is $\varphi,$𝜑, which takes the value r + 0 = r, and which we want to "act like a function" in some way. One example is that we need to evaluate this at $r=0.$𝑟=0. But... Metamath is just going to replace $\varphi$𝜑 with a fixed expression, and it has to be that same expression at every occurrence in our formula! We can't say "at this point, unify $\varphi$𝜑 with |- 0 + 0 = 0, but then later on try to unify it with |- S t + 0 = S t."

How do we solve this? First off, we realize that $\varphi$𝜑 can only be unified with one thing, so in the example case we've been using we say that phi is the wff x + 0 = x. How do we ensure that this doesn't get unified with 0 + 0 = 0 once, ruining our ability to substitute other values of $x$𝑥? The answer is that x cannot be of typecode num.

We instead do a very clever trick. At least I think it's very clever. We make the following definitions:

metamath
$c var $.
$v x $.
vx $f var x $.
nx $a num x $.
xzero $p |- ( x = 0 -> ( x + 0 ) = x ) $=
  vx nx n0 weq vx nx n0 nadd n0 vx nx vx nx n0 weq vx nx n0 nadd n0 n0 nadd n0
  vx nx n0 n0 eq-add-left n0 n0 nadd n0 weq vx nx n0 weq n0 add-0l a1i eq-trd
  vx nx n0 eq-sym eq-trd $.

We put $x=0$𝑥=0 in our antecedent, and then use the axioms of our arithmetic to "perform the substitution". The axioms of our arithmetic are usable because we're always allowed to convert var x to num x via nx. Yet we can never unify, say, S 0 in place of x because we would need var S 0, which we can never build.

I absolutely love this, because it's very instructive for us on two counts: It gives us a Metamath programming trick which we can use, which is essentially the same notion as an object variable in logic. And it gives us a method to construct our axiom systems (our axiom systems must be sufficiently powerful that we can apply the operations above which should be interpreted as "substituting the variable for zero"). I think that's a very general lesson about theory building.

Introducing a var leads us to one other piece of machinery, whose construction requires the use of disjoint variables.

Suppose we introduce vars and we manage to prove the theorem |- x + 0 = x. We wouldn't actually be able to use this to prove |- t + 0 = t, because t is a num. We can't allow t to be converted into a var either, as this would lead us straight back to the problem that we just fixed. No issue, we just apply our trick of "evaluating at a variable" again. We introduced the axiom $a num x $. to deal with vars, what's one more axiom?

Let $\varphi$𝜑 be our wff which we think of as a function of x. And $\psi$𝜓 is the value of x as the num t that we want to plug in. Then we just create an axiom saying we can plug in that value:

metamath
${ 
  vtonum.1 $e |- ( x = t -> ( ph <-> ps ) ) $.
  vtonum.2 $e |- ph $.
  vtonum $a |- ps $.
$}

It looks good, but in fact we can now prove |- 1 = 0. Why? Take ph := x = x, t := 0, ps := x = 0. We conclude |- x = 0. A second application of vtonum with ph := x = 0, t := 1, and ps := 1 = 0 concludes |- 1 = 0.

What went wrong? In our first application of vtonum, an $x$𝑥 was leftover in our formula even though we were supposed to be substituting for $x$𝑥! The problem is that we allowed the variable $x$𝑥 to occur anywhere in $\psi$𝜓. This is exactly what the disjoint variable restriction, $d ... $. prevents:

metamath
${ 
  $d x ps $. $d x t $.
  vtonum.1 $e |- ( x = t -> ( ph <-> ps ) ) $.
  vtonum.2 $e |- ph $.
  vtonum $a |- ps $.
$}

TODO: comment on the reason for the t variable restriction.

Compare this to a similar theorem inside set.mm, vtocl.

Now, we can begin to construct our induction axiom. Well, we have a formula $\varphi$𝜑 which is our $P(x)$𝑃(𝑥). We have a base case, $P(0)$𝑃(0) which I will call $\psi$𝜓. The conclusion of our rule, $P(t)$𝑃(𝑡) which can be evaluated at any num t, I'll call $\tau.$𝜏. The naming and ordering of the essential hypotheses is something we inherit from set.mm by the way, don't blame me for it!3

metamath
${
  $d x ps $. $d x ta $. $d x t $.
  ax-ind.at-zero $e |- ( x = 0 -> ( ph <-> ps ) ) $.
  ...
  ax-ind.at-num $e |- ( x = t ->  ( ph <-> ta ) ) $.
  ax-ind.base $e |- ps $.
  ...
  ax-ind $a |- ta $.
$}

Okay, and of course we also don't want our expression for $P(t)\to P(t+1)$𝑃(𝑡)→𝑃(𝑡+1) to unify with some specific num t, so that has to be a var as well. So we evaluate at y (call the wff $\chi$𝜒), evaluate at y+1 (call the wff $\theta$𝜃), and have our inductive hypothesis $(\chi\to\theta)$(𝜒→𝜃).

metamath
${
  $d x ps $. $d x ta $. $d x t $. 
  $d x ch $. $d x th $. 
  $d x y $. $d y ph $.
  ax-ind.at-zero $e |- ( x = 0 -> ( ph <-> ps ) ) $.
  ax-ind.at-var $e |- ( x = y -> ( ph <-> ch ) ) $.
  ax-ind.at-succvar $e |- ( x = S y -> ( ph <-> th ) ) $.
  ax-ind.at-num $e |- ( x = t ->  ( ph <-> ta ) ) $.
  ax-ind.base $e |- ps $.
  ax-ind.step $e |- ( ch -> th ) $.
  ax-ind $a |- ta $.
$}

And there we have it, our axiom of induction!

5.1.4 Equality: two more axioms, three more constructions

Warning

This section is AI-generated and has not been reviewed.

So far the quantifier only moves implications around. Tarski's system has two more axioms for a language whose only predicate is equality:

metamath
${
  $d x y $.
  ax6v $a |- -. A. x -. x = y $.
$}

ax-7 $a |- ( x = y -> ( x = z -> y = z ) ) $.

ax6v says that something equals y: it is not the case that every x differs from it. ax-7 says that two things equal to the same thing are equal to each other. Together with ax-gen, ax-4, and ax-5 these are all of Tarski's predicate axioms for this language; set.mm adds ax-8 and ax-9 for membership. Our ax6v is Axiom B7 of Tarski, which requires x and y to be distinct. In set.mm the axiom ax-6 has no such condition and ax6v is a theorem that weakens it.

Prove these in order. They need nothing beyond chapter 04 and 5.1.2.

metamath
${
  $d x y $.
  equid $p |- x = x $= ? $.
$}

equcomi $p |- ( x = y -> y = x ) $= ? $.

${
  $d ph x $.
  $d x y $.
  spvw $p |- ( A. x ph -> ph ) $= ? $.
$}

equid has a distinctness condition on a variable that does not occur in its statement: y is a dummy variable used inside the proof. After proving it, compare

text
show statement equid /full
show statement spvw /full

Metamath lists <x,y> as an optional pair in both. A mandatory pair constrains everyone who applies the theorem; an optional pair only constrains the proof. equcomi applies equid and needs no $d at all.

spvw removes a quantifier that binds nothing. Even this needs ax6v: in an empty domain A. x ph would hold for every ph. Its converse is ax-5, so the two implications of 19.3v are now both available. We have no <-> here to state them as one equivalence.

Hints

  • equid: this one is longer than the others. Specialize ax-7 to ( y = x -> ( y = x -> x = x ) ) and contract with pm2.43i. Contrapose with con3i and lift over y with alimi. ax-5 on -. x = x gives the missing first link for syl; this is the step using $d x y $.. Contrapose with con1i and detach ax6v.
  • equcomi: this one should be quick. Specialize ax-7 with z := x, swap the antecedents with com12, and detach equid.
  • spvw: aim for ( A. x ph -> ( -. ph -> A. x -. x = y ) ) using pm2.21, al2imi, ax-5 on -. ph, syl, and com12. ax6v refutes the inner consequent, so pm2.21i and imim2i change it to ph. Finish with pm2.18.

For a third rejection experiment, delete $d x y $. from the completed equid. make -C ai/predicate-calculus invalid writes that variant too:

bash
metamath 'read "ai/predicate-calculus/invalid/missing-dummy-pair.mm"' 'verify proof equid' exit

This is where we stop building. Bound-variable change, the existential quantifier, and anything stated with <->, /\, or \/ need a lot more groundwork, and set.mm has already done it. We continue there.

biimpi and biimpri extract the two implications from an equivalence; impbii joins them. simpl and simpr extract conjuncts; jca combines two consequences of one antecedent. orc and olc introduce disjunction, while jaoi combines its two cases. For example, applying alimi to simpl transports conjunction elimination through a universal quantifier.

We are passing over many inference, deduction, and closed variants, along with specialized rearrangements. A deduction rule retains a common antecedent in its premises and conclusion; an inference rule consumes established assertions. syl and con3i are familiar examples of reusable inference tools.

Specialization sp is a derived theorem. Its construction, and the equality machinery supporting substitution, are available to inspect. Nonfreeness can also be expressed by explicit hypotheses rather than the stronger syntactic nonoccurrence condition $d x ph $.. The bound-variable rules below expose both kinds of interface. No set-theoretic reasoning is needed for our targets.
