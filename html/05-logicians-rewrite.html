5 Talking to Logicians

Everything in chapter 04 was a $p statement that the chapter 03 verifier accepted. A logic textbook is mostly a different kind of object: English claims about what can and can't be proved. "MU is not derivable." "Any proof from a hypothesis can be converted into a proof of an implication." "This system is consistent."

None of those is a $p in any file we have. This chapter learns the vocabulary, the notation, and the history anyway, because that is how logicians talk. The one rule is that every claim is labelled by what the verifier has actually seen.

5.1 Ground rules

5.1.1 Three tags

Tag The claim is... How it gets upgraded
[checked] "this file verifies", and it does. Nothing to do.
[IOU] that one specific $p has a proof, which nobody has written down yet. Write the proof, run the verifier.
[folk] about all proofs, or about the absence of a proof. Needs a new database in which proofs are themselves the objects (section 5.8).

"Folk" doesn't mean doubtful. Every folk claim in this chapter is standard, and I'd be astonished if any were wrong. The tag records how much of it the verifier has seen, which is none of it. Until section 5.8 is carried out, metatheorem is itself a folk word: it's what logicians call these claims, and we have no definition of it.

5.1.2 Two kinds of folk claim

Recipes. "Any proof of this shape can be turned into a proof of that shape." The deduction theorem, cut elimination, completeness, and "these two axiom systems prove the same things" are all recipes.

A recipe never has to be believed. Each use of it is an IOU: apply the recipe to the one proof you care about, and check the result. A program that implements the recipe is an untrusted helper; only its output goes through the verifier. If the program is wrong, the verifier says so.

Barriers. "No proof of X exists." MU, consistency, "Peirce's law doesn't follow from ax-1 and ax-2" are barriers. There is no IOU to cash. A search that fails is not something the verifier can look at.

So the stubborn position has a comfortable half and an uncomfortable half. Recipes can be tolerated as folk knowledge indefinitely, because they pay out in checked proofs whenever asked. Barriers are where the real debt is.

5.1.3 The closure clause

Why are barriers different? A .mm file lists the steps that are allowed. Nowhere does it say "and nothing else is a theorem." That sentence is a fact about the verifier, not a statement in the file. Adding an $a can only make more things provable, so no $a added to miu.mm could ever say that M U is unprovable.

Every barrier argument in this chapter has the same four parts:

  1. Pick a property P of strings.
  2. Check that each rule, given premises with P, produces a conclusion with P. This is a finite check, one per rule.
  3. Check that the target string lacks P.
  4. Closure: theorems arise only by applying the rules, so every theorem has P, so the target is not a theorem.

Parts 1 to 3 are concrete. Part 4 is induction on derivations, and it is the piece of folk reasoning this whole chapter leans on. Recipes need it too, but only to argue that they work on every input, never for one use.

This also says what formalizing a barrier requires: a database where "theorem of system S" is defined as the smallest set of strings closed under S's rules, so that part 4 becomes a provable induction principle.

5.1.4 "How do you know your checker is right?"

You don't. You shrink what has to be trusted until it is small enough to stare at, and you write a second one independently (chapter 03). This is the de Bruijn criterion: a proof system should emit proof objects that a small, separately written program can check. The same question applies to a referee's brain; the difference is that the checker's trusted part is written down. See Pollack, "How to believe a machine-checked proof" (1998).

In conversation, the position of these notes is roughly: "formal proofs are finite strings checked by a mechanical procedure; I accept simple combinatorial arguments about strings informally, and I treat the rest of the metatheory as unformalized until it is machine-checked." Logicians will recognize this as close to Hilbert's formalism, with finitary reasoning as the accepted informal part. It is a respectable position with a long history, which is next.

5.2 History

Dates are for orientation. Each entry says what it left behind in our files or our vocabulary.

  • Frege, Begriffsschrift (1879). The first formal system in the modern sense: axioms plus modus ponens. ax-1 and ax-2 are his. The turnstile ⊢ descends from his "judgment stroke."
  • Whitehead and Russell, Principia Mathematica (1910–13). The source of labels like pm2.43 (theorem ∗2.43), and of the fear of "wasting your undergrad proving 1+1=2."
  • Hilbert's program (1920s). Treat proofs as finite strings and study them with elementary, finitary reasoning. Hilbert called this Metamathematik, the origin of the word. A Hilbert system is one with many axioms and very few rules, like chapter 04's. Textbook: Hilbert and Ackermann (1928).
  • Post (1921, 1943). Truth tables, and the first proof that the propositional calculus proves exactly the tautologies. Later, Post canonical systems: pure string-rewriting rules. MIU is one, and a Metamath database is close to one.
  • Łukasiewicz (around 1930). The three axioms ax-1, ax-2, ax-3, trimmed down from Frege's six. Meredith's single axiom is from 1953.
  • Herbrand, Tarski (1930). The deduction theorem.
  • Gödel (1930, 1931). Completeness of predicate logic. Then incompleteness, whose method is the one section 5.8 needs: encode formulas and proofs as data inside a system, so the system can talk about them.
  • Tarski (1933). The object language / metalanguage split, and a definition of truth in a structure. ⊨ belongs to this tradition.
  • Gentzen (1934–35). Natural deduction and the sequent calculus, built so that "deducible from assumptions" is itself an object the rules manipulate. Cut elimination (the Hauptsatz). Jaśkowski found natural deduction independently in 1934.
  • Church, Turing (1936). Precise definitions of "mechanical procedure", and with them "decidable."
  • Kleene, Introduction to Metamathematics (1952). Fixed much of the notation still in use, including Γ ⊢ φ. Lorenzen (1955) introduced admissible rules.
  • de Bruijn, Automath (1967 onward). The first system for writing complete proofs that a computer checks. Van Benthem Jutting checked Landau's Grundlagen der Analysis in it (1977). The "de Bruijn criterion" was named later by Barendregt.
  • Mizar (1973), LCF (Milner, 1972 onward), Boyer–Moore. LCF's idea is the recipe idea of 5.1.2: arbitrary untrusted programs may construct proofs, but only a small kernel can mint a theorem. Shankar checked Gödel's incompleteness theorem in the Boyer–Moore prover in 1986, an early case of a barrier-style result fully machine-checked.
  • Megill, Metamath (1990s). Tarski's 1965 predicate calculus without "free variable" side conditions, plus Megill's own axioms (1995), in a system whose only built-in operation is substitution.
  • Checkers checking checkers (2006 onward). Harrison, "Towards self-verification of HOL Light" (2006). Carneiro, "Models for Metamath" (2016) and Metamath Zero (2019), designed so that its verifier can be specified and proved correct in its own logic.

5.3 A dictionary

Left column: what a logician says. Right column: the nearest thing in our files. The definitions are for recognition, not for proving anything.

5.3.1 Words

Logician's term Meaning In our files
object language The formal strings being studied. What appears after the typecode in a statement.
metalanguage The language used to talk about them (usually English). Comments, and these notes.
formula, wff A string built by the grammar. Anything with a wff syntax proof.
schema, metavariable A pattern standing for all its instances. Every statement with ph in it. Textbooks also have fixed atoms p, q; set.mm has none.
rule of inference Premises and a conclusion. An $a with $e hypotheses, such as ax-mp.
Hilbert system Many axioms, few rules. Chapter 04's database.
derivation, deduction, proof A finite sequence or tree of formulas, each justified by a rule. The RPN proof after $=, which is that tree in postorder.
theorem A formula with a derivation. A $p that verifies.
derivable rule The conclusion can be derived from the premises. A $p with $e hypotheses. [checked] when it verifies.
admissible rule Whenever the premises are theorems, so is the conclusion. Nothing. A recipe at best.
metatheorem A claim about all derivations of a system. Nothing yet (5.8).
valuation, truth assignment A choice of true/false for each atom. Nothing yet.
tautology True under every valuation. Nothing yet.
sound Every theorem is a tautology. Folk. The standard source of barriers.
complete Every tautology is a theorem. Folk recipe.
consistent Some formula is not a theorem. Folk barrier.
decidable A program answers "theorem or not?" and always halts. Recipe for "yes", barrier for "no".
independent An axiom can't be derived from the others. Folk barrier.
finitary Elementary reasoning about finite strings; later pinned down as primitive recursive arithmetic (PRA). The kind of informal argument these notes accept.
proof theory, model theory The study of derivations; the study of structures and truth. This chapter is nearly all proof theory.

Two false friends. In set.mm, "deduction form" (suffix d) means the shared-antecedent convention, and "inference" means a rule with bare hypotheses; a logician's "deduction" is just a derivation from assumptions. And some authors call every schema a "metatheorem", which is a much weaker sense than the one above.

5.3.2 Symbols

  • ⊢ φ: "φ is a theorem." This is a metalanguage sentence. Metamath's |- is a declared constant at the front of a string. They are related by convention only.
  • Γ ⊢ φ: "φ is derivable from the assumptions Γ." A $p with $e hypotheses is the checked analogue for a finite Γ of schemas. Enderton writes Γ; α for adding one assumption.
  • Γ ⊨ φ: every valuation making all of Γ true makes φ true. A different claim from ⊢; soundness and completeness are the two directions between them. The |= in hol.mm is unrelated: there it is a constant separating a context from a conclusion.
  • Rule notation: premises above a line, conclusion below.
text
  φ      φ → ψ
 ───────────────  MP
        ψ
  • Arrows: → is our ->. Older books write ⊃. ⇒ can be the sequent separator, Mendelson's implication, or plain English "implies". Γ ⇒ Δ and Γ ⊢ Δ are both used for sequents.
  • Γ, Δ: lists or sets of formulas. ⊥ is a constant for falsehood, which set.mm's propositional axioms do without.

Books. For a three-axiom Hilbert system like ours, Mendelson chapter 1 is the closest match. Enderton takes every tautology as an axiom, so his propositional proofs look nothing like chapter 04. For sequent calculus, Negri and von Plato, Structural Proof Theory.

5.4 Specimen: MIU, the smallest barrier

MIU is Hofstadter's toy system from Gödel, Escher, Bach. It is too simple to hold interesting proofs, which makes it a clean display case. See miu.mm (Appendix D of the Metamath book is a copy of it).

metamath
$c M I U |- wff $.
$v x y $.
wx $f wff x $.
wy $f wff y $.
we  $a wff $.
wM  $a wff x M $.
wI  $a wff x I $.
wU  $a wff x U $.
wxy $a wff x y $.
ax  $a |- M I $.
${ Ia   $e |- x I $.       I_  $a |- x I U $. $}
${ IIa  $e |- M x $.       II  $a |- M x x $. $}
${ IIIa $e |- x I I I y $. III $a |- x U y $. $}
${ IVa  $e |- x U U y $.   IV  $a |- x y $. $}

wff here just means "string": we is the empty string, wM/wI/wU append a letter, wxy concatenates. The rules: append U after a final I; double everything after the M; replace I I I by U; delete U U.

Question 1. Prove these. Both become [checked].

metamath
theorem1 $p |- M U I I U $= ? $.

${
  II2.1 $e |- M x $.
  II2 $p |- M x x x x $= ? $.
$}

Question 2. |- M I I I I I I I I is an [IOU]; cash it. Then the recipe: "for every k, M followed by 2^k Is is a theorem." Write the program that prints the proof for a given k. The claim for all k stays [folk]; each k you run is [checked].

The barrier. |- M U has no proof. [folk] In the four-part form of 5.1.3:

  1. P: the number of Is is not divisible by 3.
  2. ax has one I. I_ and IV don't change the count, II doubles it, III subtracts 3. None of these turns a non-multiple of 3 into a multiple.
  3. M U has zero Is.
  4. Closure.

Notice what the MIU language cannot do. It has no string that says "MU is unprovable." The barrier isn't a missing $p in miu.mm; it isn't a statement of miu.mm at all.

Question 3. The same shape, different outcomes:

metamath
${
  bad.1 $e |- M U $.
  bad $p |- U $= ? $.
$}

Argue (folk) that bad can't be proved: find a property that every string derivable from ax and bad.1 has, and that U lacks. Then note that the rule "from M U infer U" is nevertheless admissible, vacuously, because its premise is never a theorem. Derivable means there is a proof from the premises as $e hypotheses. Admissible only promises something when the premises are outright theorems. Derivable is a checked notion; admissible is a folk one.

Folk, for the record. The MIU theorems are exactly the strings M followed by Is and Us whose I-count is not divisible by 3. One direction is the barrier above; the other is a recipe. The Mathlib archive has all of this machine-checked in Lean, which is what a cashed-in version looks like.

5.5 Specimen: the deduction theorem, the standard recipe

If Γ; α ⊢ β, then Γ ⊢ (α → β). [folk recipe]

In our terms: a proof of ps using the hypothesis $e |- ph can be converted into a proof of |- ( ph -> ps ). The recipe rewrites every line ch of the old proof as ( ph -> ch ), and chapter 04 already built a tool for each case:

  • the line is the hypothesis ph itself: id;
  • the line is an axiom or another hypothesis: a1i;
  • the line came from ax-mp: mpd on the two rewritten lines.

The opposite direction is not folk at all. From |- ( ph -> ps ) and a hypothesis |- ph, one ax-mp gives |- ps. [checked], every time.

Megill took the same hard line about this theorem. From the Metamath book: "the Standard Deduction Theorem is invoked simply to claim that it can be done in principle, without actually doing it." set.mm never invokes it. Instead it uses deduction style: carry the antecedent ph -> on every line from the start, and prove a d version of each rule (mpd, syld, a1d, ...). That is why chapter 04 was full of i/d variants: they are the recipe, unrolled by hand, with each piece checked. See mmnatded.

Question 4. Take a chapter 04 inference with one hypothesis, say con4i or a1i, apply the recipe by hand to its proof, and verify the resulting closed theorem.

Question 5 (project). Write the recipe as a program for proofs that use only ax-1, ax-2, ax-mp, and hypotheses. Run it on several inferences from 4.1. This also answers the first TODO in 4.1.6.

Preview. With quantifiers, the recipe needs a side condition. set.mm has ax-gen: from |- ph infer |- A. x ph. Rewriting that line naively would produce ( ph -> A. x ph ), which is wrong when ph is x = y.

5.5.1 The question from 4.1.6

There I asked: if I invent a symbol => to mean "deduces" and write axioms for it, why does anyone say I still need a metatheorem?

Split the worry into its three parts.

  • Everything my => axioms prove corresponds to a real deduction. Recipe.
  • Every real deduction is reflected by a => proof. Recipe.
  • When there is no deduction, => says so. Barrier. No set of $a statements can do this, by 5.1.3: axioms only add.

So the invented arrow does capture deduction, in both directions, in the cashable sense. What it can't capture is the closure clause. That is the whole philosophical gap, and it is the same gap as in MIU. The invented arrow also already exists: it is Gentzen's.

5.6 Lab: sequent calculus

In a Hilbert system, assumptions live outside the formulas, in $e statements. Gentzen's sequent calculus (1934) puts them in the string. A sequent Γ ⇒ Δ has a list of formulas on each side, read as "if everything on the left holds, something on the right holds." This is the place where the existence of a deduction from assumptions is an ordinary object-language statement, so it is the right lab for this chapter.

5.6.1 The database

metamath
$c ( ) -> -. wff ctx |- => $.
$v ph ps ch th $.
$v G H D E $.
wph $f wff ph $.
wps $f wff ps $.
wch $f wff ch $.
wth $f wff th $.
vG $f ctx G $.
vH $f ctx H $.
vD $f ctx D $.
vE $f ctx E $.
wn $a wff -. ph $.
wi $a wff ( ph -> ps ) $.
c0 $a ctx $.
cadd $a ctx G ph $.
ax-id $a |- G ph H => D ph E $.
${
  ax-negl.1 $e |- G H => D ph E $.
  ax-negl $a |- G -. ph H => D E $.
$}
${
  ax-negr.1 $e |- G ph H => D E $.
  ax-negr $a |- G H => D -. ph E $.
$}
${
  ax-impl.1 $e |- G H => D ph E $.
  ax-impl.2 $e |- G ps H => D E $.
  ax-impl $a |- G ( ph -> ps ) H => D E $.
$}
${
  ax-impr.1 $e |- G ph H => D ps E $.
  ax-impr $a |- G H => D ( ph -> ps ) E $.
$}

A ctx is a list of wffs: c0 is empty, cadd appends one. No commas are needed, since every wff is a variable, starts with -., or is wrapped in parentheses. G ph H means "a list with ph somewhere in it", so each rule works at any position.

Each connective has a left rule (how to use it as an assumption) and a right rule (how to prove it). ax-id closes a goal when the same formula appears on both sides. A worked example, with all contexts empty:

metamath
seqid $p |- => ( ph -> ph ) $=
  wph wph c0 c0 c0 c0 wph c0 c0 c0 c0 ax-id ax-impr $.

The syntax proof of the two-formula context ph ps is wps wph c0 cadd cadd: the formula goes on the stack before the context it is appended to. mmj2 expects every |- statement to parse as a wff, so use metamath.exe or the chapter 03 checker.

Question 6. Prove the three Hilbert axioms, Peirce's law, and double negation elimination.

metamath
seqax1 $p |- => ( ph -> ( ps -> ph ) ) $= ? $.

seqax2 $p |- => ( ( ph -> ( ps -> ch ) ) -> ( ( ph -> ps ) -> ( ph -> ch ) ) ) $= ? $.

seqax3 $p |- => ( ( -. ph -> -. ps ) -> ( ps -> ph ) ) $= ? $.

seqpeirce $p |- => ( ( ( ph -> ps ) -> ph ) -> ph ) $= ? $.

seqnotnotr $p |- => ( -. -. ph -> ph ) $= ? $.

Hint: work from the goal backwards. Pick any compound formula and apply its rule; the premises are smaller. Stop when a formula appears on both sides. There is nothing to be clever about, which is the point: compare seqpeirce with the effort peirce took in 4.1.5.

5.6.2 The trade

ax-impr moves an assumption from the left of => into an antecedent. That is the deduction theorem, and here it is a primitive rule. So what did it cost?

Question 7. Try to prove modus ponens:

metamath
${
  seqmp.1 $e |- => ph $.
  seqmp.2 $e |- => ( ph -> ps ) $.
  seqmp $p |- => ps $= ? $.
$}

Look at the conclusion of each rule. Which could end in => ps? Write down your argument that none can, and notice that it is a barrier argument: it uses closure.

The cost was modus ponens. Each system makes one of the two primitive and leaves the other as folk knowledge, or pays to have both:

System Where assumptions live Discharging an assumption Modus ponens
Hilbert (4.1) $e hypotheses folk recipe (5.5) primitive: ax-mp
set.mm deduction style an antecedent on every line not needed checked: mpd
hol.mm a context term in every statement checked checked: mpd, from ax-eqmp
sequent calculus, as above left of => primitive: ax-impr folk recipe
sequent calculus with cut left of => primitive: ax-impr checked: Question 8

Cut is the rule that buys modus ponens back:

metamath
${
  ax-cut.1 $e |- G => D ph $.
  ax-cut.2 $e |- H ph => E $.
  ax-cut $a |- G H => D E $.
$}

Question 8. With ax-cut added, prove seqmp. Hint: first prove |- ph ( ph -> ps ) => ps with one ax-impl and two ax-ids. Cut it against seqmp.2, then against seqmp.1.

Question 9 (project). Questions 6 and 8 are the ingredients of a recipe: every theorem of chapter 04's system has a sequent proof using cut. Replace each ax-1, ax-2, ax-3 step by the matching seqax proof and each ax-mp by seqmp. Write the translator and run it on id and peirce from myset.mm.

5.6.3 Search, and where truth tables come from

Working backwards through any rule except cut removes one connective. So backward search always halts, and it ends in one of two ways.

  • Every branch closes with ax-id. The search has written a proof. Each success is [checked]. As a recipe: "every tautology has a cut-free proof" (completeness).
  • Some branch reaches only variables, with none shared between the sides, such as ph ps => ch. No rule applies. Read it as a truth assignment: left side true, right side false. That assignment makes the original formula false.

So valuations are not an extra idea imported from outside; they are what failed branches look like. And a falsifying assignment is a small, concrete certificate. What it certifies is "not a tautology." Getting from there to "not provable" is soundness, which is a barrier argument with P = "true under every assignment". The certificate is parts 1 to 3 of 5.1.3. Part 4 is still closure.

Question 10 (project). Write the backward search for this database, printing proofs the checker accepts on success and an assignment on failure. The SICP query language from chapter 02 is a natural fit.

Question 11 (project, harder). Compose the search with a translator from cut-free sequent proofs back to Hilbert proofs, reading ph ps => ch as ( ph -> ( ps -> ch ) ) (extra right-hand formulas become negated antecedents) and proving one deduction-form lemma per sequent rule. The result is a program that proves any propositional tautology in myset.mm. That cashes the completeness theorem for chapter 04's system, settles the third TODO in 4.1.6, and retires the remaining hand proofs of 4.1.4 and 4.1.5.

Question 12. Use the three-part certificate on a different barrier: Peirce's law is not provable from ax-1, ax-2, and ax-mp alone. [folk] Take three truth values 0 < ½ < 1, and let a -> b be 1 when a ≤ b and b otherwise (give -. any table you like). Check by computation that ax-1 and ax-2 always evaluate to 1, that ax-mp preserves "always 1", and that Peirce's law takes the value ½ at ph = ½, ps = 0. Which of the four parts did you just do, and which is missing?

5.6.4 Derivable versus admissible, again

Question 13. Weakening adds an unused assumption:

metamath
${
  wk.1 $e |- G => D $.
  wk $p |- G ph => D $= ? $.
$}

Argue that this has no proof: which rule's conclusion could match? Then describe the recipe that makes weakening admissible: given an actual proof of a concrete sequent, add the extra formula to every line. The recipe needs the proof behind the premise. A $e hypothesis hides it. That is the precise difference between the two words.

Cut elimination (Gentzen's Hauptsatz): any proof using ax-cut, with no extra $e hypotheses, can be converted to one without it. [folk recipe] In other words, cut is admissible in the cut-free system, though by Question 7 it is not derivable. Two things follow, both folk:

  • Subformula property. A cut-free proof contains only pieces of its final sequent. This is why the search of 5.6.3 doesn't have to guess anything.
  • Consistency of chapter 04's system. If it proved both ph and -. ph, then Question 9 and two cuts would give a proof of the empty sequent |- =>. Cut elimination would give a cut-free one. But no rule's conclusion is empty. This is a barrier assembled from two recipes and one inspection, and it never mentions truth tables.

Preview. With quantifiers, the ∀-right rule requires that x not occur in the context, which in Metamath is a $d between x and each of G H D E. Without it the rule proves ph ⇒ ∀x ph, the same mistake as the ax-gen preview in 5.5.

5.8 Paying off barriers, later

To turn a barrier into a $p, proofs of the system under study have to be objects in some other database, with the closure clause as a definition. This needs quantifiers and at least a little set theory or arithmetic, so it waits until after set.mm proper. Things to look at when the time comes:

  • set.mm already contains Metamath. Carneiro's "Metamath formal systems" section formalizes Appendix C of the Metamath book. Its mCls (see df-mcls) is defined as an intersection of all sets closed under the axioms and substitution, which is exactly the closure clause; mThm (df-mthm) is the set of theorems. A barrier for MIU would be a set.mm theorem of the form "this string is not in mThm of that system."
  • set.mm also contains Gödel-style encodings of first-order formulas as sets (df-goal, df-fmla, df-sat, df-prv), in the same author's section on model theory.
  • Solovay's peano.mm separates its formal statements from its informal metamathematics in the header, and adds: "All our metamathematical arguments are quite constructive and certainly could be formalized in Primitive Recursive Arithmetic." That names a target far weaker than ZFC. Barriers like MU need only induction over finite strings.
  • Metamath Zero is the same idea pushed to the verifier itself.
  • The Lean MIU development, for a complete small example in another system.

Two things to expect, both folk for now.

The regress stops at an inspection. A set.mm theorem about MIU is a theorem about a set-theoretic definition. That the definition matches miu.mm is checked by reading both. This is the same kind of act as trusting that the verifier implements substitution, and it is small.

The tower has no top. A barrier about system S is proved in a stronger system T, and T's own consistency is a new barrier. Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says that, for systems containing enough arithmetic, T can't supply that one itself. The practical consequence is modest: barriers about tiny systems such as MIU or propositional calculus need very little, and that little is what Hilbert called finitary.
